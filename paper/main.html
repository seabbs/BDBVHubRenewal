Introduction

NotePlan
  • The gap.
    • Real-time case counts are incomplete because of reporting delays.
    • Generic forecasting models do not use the reporting structure, so they treat incomplete recent counts as final.
    • Key references: (Höhle and Heiden 2014; Johnson et al. 2025; Reich et al. 2022).
  • Context.
    • Nowcasting methods model reporting delays directly.
    • Time series foundation models forecast new series with no training on them.
    • Training on domain-specific synthetic data has precedent: KernelSynth for Chronos (Ansari et al. 2024), ForecastPFN (Dooley et al. 2023), prior-fitted networks (Müller et al. 2022; Hollmann et al. 2025), and Mantis for outbreaks (Dudley et al. 2026).
    • Key references: (McGough et al. 2020; Günther et al. 2021; Wolffram et al. 2023; Ansari et al. 2024; Das, Kong, et al. 2024; Woo et al. 2024; Rasul et al. 2023; Garza, Challu, and Mergenthaler-Canseco 2023; Aksu et al. 2024; Shchur et al. 2025; Kalahasti et al. 2026; Dudley et al. 2025).
  • What we did.
    • We fine-tuned Chronos-2, a general time series foundation model, on simulated outbreaks rather than training a model from scratch.
    • We evaluated it on held-out simulations, on the German COVID-19 hospitalisation nowcast hub, and prospectively on the Ebola forecasting hub.
    • Key references: (Ansari et al. 2025; Johnson et al. 2025).

Methods

Data

NotePlan
  • We used the German COVID-19 hospitalisation nowcast hub’s reporting triangle, nationally, by state and by age group.
  • We used the Ebola forecasting hub’s weekly confirmed cases by symptom onset, with every release, by country, province and health zone.
  • Key references: (Wolffram et al. 2023; KIT Metrics Lab et al. 2025; InsightNet-US 2026; MacNeil et al. 2010).

Baselines

NotePlan
  • We compare with randomwalk, a random walk with no delay adjustment, and randomwalknowcast, a random walk nowcast.
  • The SI describes both in full.
  • Key references: (Johnson et al. 2025).

Foundation model setup and fine-tuning

NotePlan
  • We give Chronos-2 the reporting triangle as series, one per place and reporting delay, each missing until reported (Figure 1B).
  • Chronos-2 is built from attention layers, in which each series updates what it has learned as a weighted mix of the other series, with more weight on those it finds relevant.
  • Without more information, the model has no marker of which delay series belong to which place’s count.
  • We add learned attention biases that nudge these weights by how two series are related (the same series, the same place, or a place and its parent or child), building on Moirai’s any-variate attention and TableFormer’s relation biases (Woo et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2022) (SI).
  • A third each of the training blocks with places have a country equal to, above or later than the sum of its places.
  • We fine-tune base Chronos-2 on synthetic data on a laptop, with its default LoRA recipe.
  • LoRA trains a small update to about 1% of the model’s weights rather than retraining them all, so fine-tuning starts from the original model (SI).
  • The learning rate falls linearly to zero at 8,000 simulations.
  • Each simulation gives 32 training blocks.
  • A block is daily, 3-day or weekly, or daily with one weekly or 3-day total row per place.
  • Four in five blocks are multivariate and one in five univariate.
  • We save checkpoints at 250, 500, 750, 1,000, 2,000, 4,000 and 8,000 simulations.
  • We report the final checkpoint, at 8,000 simulations, where the learning rate reaches zero, and select no checkpoint on real data.
  • Training draws simulations from a balanced, shuffled design, so every checkpoint has seen the whole grid.
  • Chronos-2 forecasts the country, provinces and health zones as separate series in one joint block.
  • The copula only pairs draws, for joint scores and sums, and leaves marginals unchanged.
  • We compare the joint block with forecasting each level alone and with summing the lowest level (SI).
  • We compare lags capped at 4 weeks or 28 days with lags run to the truth delay (SI).
  • We compare the model with attention biases with one fine-tuned without them (SI).
  • We fine-tune Chronos-2 on complete counts only, without delay series, and compare it with the ragged model (SI).
  • We fine-tune Chronos-2 on real data and compare it with the model fine-tuned on simulations (SI).
  • We compare the fine-tuned model with zero-shot Chronos-2 and with Chronos-2 given only complete data.
  • We plot each checkpoint’s scores, with both as reference lines (SI).
  • Figure 1 shows the simulation, the ragged series, training and forecasting.

  • Key references: (Ansari et al. 2025, 2024; Hu et al. 2021; Das, Faw, et al. 2024).

Synthetic data generation

NotePlan
  • We simulate outbreaks of up to 52 weeks with epinowcast over a crossed grid of epidemic process, trajectory, places and age groups, reporting delays and their changes, report days, reporting shocks and observation noise (SI).
  • The outbreaks range from one wave to several, with cases spread over many places or concentrated in a few, delays that drift, step, bunch or slow down, and NB1 or Poisson noise.
  • We checked the grid’s ranges against published values for several diseases, the case studies among them.
  • Key references: (Abbott et al. 2026; Lison et al. 2024; Park et al. 2024; Ansari et al. 2024; Dooley et al. 2023; Müller et al. 2022; Hollmann et al. 2025).

Correlated trajectories

Chronos-2 draws each place and week on its own. randomwalknowcast draws each nowcast week on its own, and its forecast weeks follow its walk within each sample. Paired at random, independent draws give sums over weeks or places that are too narrow. We pair them into trajectories with a Gaussian copula over weeks and places. Each trajectory draws a vector \(z\) from a multivariate normal with correlation matrix \(\Sigma\). Each place-week’s draws are then reordered across trajectories so that they rank as \(z\) does. This is an empirical copula reordering in the style of the Schaake shuffle (Clark et al. 2004) and ensemble copula coupling (Schefzik, Thorarinsdottir, and Gneiting 2013). Each place-week keeps its own draws, so every marginal forecast and every per-week score is unchanged. For randomwalknowcast the reordering replaces the walk’s own dependence over weeks. \(\Sigma\) is a place correlation times a week correlation. Two steps \(\delta\) days apart have correlation \(\rho_t^{\delta/7}\). Two provinces have correlation \(\rho_p\), and the country and a province have correlation \(\rho_n\). The daily models also pair the days of a week, with correlation \(\rho_d^{|d - d'|}\) between days \(d\) and \(d'\). \(\rho_d = 0.985\) is fixed rather than estimated, as each matured error is of a week’s sum. It gives the best weekly WIS, median bias and coverage of the daily models on the Ebola release backtest. Where the country is the sum of its provinces, as for randomwalknowcast, it is summed again from the paired provinces. Copulas have been used before to give joint forecasts of seasonal influenza (Ray and Reich 2018; Brooks et al. 2018).

At each forecast origin we estimate the three correlations from the model’s own matured errors. For each forecast made at an earlier origin whose truth had been released by this origin, we take the randomised probability integral transform (PIT) of the truth in the forecast’s draws and map it to a normal score. We use the scores of the 8 latest matured weeks. Each correlation is the Pearson correlation of pairs of scores from the same forecast. We shrink it towards a fixed prior, \((\rho_t, \rho_p, \rho_n) = (0.9, 0.7, 0.7)\), with the prior worth 100 pairs, and keep it between 0 and 0.95. An origin with no matured errors takes the prior. We score the paired trajectories with the variogram score of order 0.5 (Scheuerer and Hamill 2015) on \(\log(x + 1)\) counts and the coverage of sums over weeks and over places. We also report the energy score (Gneiting et al. 2008), which has been used for epidemic trajectories from forecast hubs (Bay et al. 2026) but discriminates poorly between dependence structures (Pinson and Tastu 2013). Both scores are proper for joint forecasts (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). The SI gives the copula in full, its estimator and window, and the scores of paired against unpaired trajectories.

Evaluation on held-out synthetic data

NotePlan
  • We held out a set of simulations from development.
  • The fine-tune never trained on the held-out set’s seeds (SI).
  • We set each tuned choice on development data, apart from every scored set (SI).
  • We score sample forecasts with CRPS and log-scale CRPS, bias, and 50% and 90% coverage, relative to randomwalknowcast.
  • We score samples with the fair CRPS and the fair energy score, which correct for the number of samples, so forecasts with different numbers of samples, ours and the hub teams’, compare fairly (Ferro 2014; Zamo and Naveau 2018; Zanetta and Allen 2024).
  • We score against the data as reported \(D\) days later, with \(D\) each outbreak’s 98% reporting delay.
  • Key references: (Bracher et al. 2021; Bosse et al. 2023; Gneiting and Raftery 2007; Gneiting, Balabdaoui, and Raftery 2007; Sherratt et al. 2023).

Case study 1: German nowcast hub

NotePlan
  • We compare with the hub’s models, relative to the hub’s baseline, and evaluate as the hub did.
  • We score quantile forecasts with WIS and log-scale WIS, bias, and coverage, against the data as reported 40 days later (Wolffram et al. 2023).
  • We score each model on its own quantiles at the hub’s seven levels, interpolated linearly where it lacks one.
  • We score models that give samples, and sums over days, on the quantiles of 1,000 draws with common random numbers.
  • One fine-tuned Chronos-2 model forecasts daily, 3-day and weekly data.
  • For both case studies, we also score our models’ daily counts directly from the daily rows, against randomwalknowcast (SI).
  • We split the nowcast dates in half at 10 February 2022.
  • We developed the setup on the first half and score the second half.
  • Key references: (Wolffram et al. 2023; Johnson et al. 2025; Amaral et al. 2025).

Case study 2: prospective submission to the Ebola forecasting hub

NotePlan
  • We compare with the other submissions, relative to the hub’s baseline.
  • We score the hub’s rounds from 21 November 2026.
  • We used earlier rounds and the release backtest from earlier releases for development (SI).
  • We score sample forecasts with CRPS and log-scale CRPS, bias, and coverage, with \(D\) the 98% point of the hub data’s reporting delay.
  • We score our models on 1,000 draws with common random numbers, and hub submissions on their 100 submitted trajectories.
  • We score our submitted models on their submissions.
  • Each other model of ours forecasts a round from the last release before the round closed.
  • The fine-tuned models use the final run’s newest checkpoint (pending), trained on simulations only.
  • Key references: (InsightNet-US 2026; Viboud et al. 2018; Funk et al. 2019).

Discussion

We used one foundation model, Chronos-2, because it forecasts a group of related series in one block and takes known-future covariates, which the reporting triangle laid out as ragged series needs. In the SI we compare fine-tuning on simulations with fine-tuning on real data, on the German held-out half and on the scored BDBV rounds. BDBV is scored from the 21 November 2026 round onward, from data released before each round closed. The fine-tuned model is trained on simulations only, so it never sees the BDBV truth it is scored against.

NotePlan
  • Summary of what we did.
  • Strengths and weaknesses.
    • We used one foundation model, one source of synthetic data and two case studies.
    • We did not compare with epinowcast, or, for the synthetic study, with the model that generated the data.
    • We evaluated on a retrospective and a real-time case study, with synthetic data that can be tuned to a setting.
    • Fine-tuning ran on a laptop, so it needs no shared compute.
    • The BDBV hub imputes missing onset dates from reporting delays, which can over-smooth its data (Methods, Data) (InsightNet-US 2026).
    • Smoothed release histories mismatch the epinowcast reporting structure our simulations assume, which may disadvantage the fine-tuned Chronos-2.
    • We fine-tuned on blocks of at most 364 steps of history but forecast with all available data.
    • We did not test whether this affects Chronos-2’s handling of longer histories, which matters most for long series such as the German daily data.
    • Fine-tuning used the laptop’s GPU, which made it several times faster than its CPU.
    • On a laptop CPU alone it would run in hours.
    • Key references: (Kalahasti et al. 2026; Meyer et al. 2025).
  • Domain knowledge through synthetic data.
    • Domain knowledge enters through the simulations.
    • Whether this improves the forecasts is to be filled in from the results.
    • Key references: (Dudley et al. 2026, 2025; Dooley et al. 2023; Hollmann et al. 2025; Ansari et al. 2025).
  • Against the literature.
    • Other uses of foundation models in infectious disease modelling. Key references: (Kalahasti et al. 2026; Cai, Huang, and Xu 2026; Wang et al. 2025; Jafari et al. 2026; Du et al. 2025; Li et al. 2025; Aygün et al. 2025; Martinson et al. 2026).
    • Fine-tuning in other domains. Key references: (Laglil et al. 2026; Das, Faw, et al. 2024; Hu et al. 2021).
    • Other nowcasting approaches. Key references: (Bastos et al. 2019; Seaman et al. 2022; Bergström et al. 2022; Sumalinab et al. 2024; Schneble et al. 2021; Kassteele, Eilers, and Wallinga 2019; Overton et al. 2023; Mellor et al. 2025; Tang et al. 2025; Stoner, Economou, and Halliday 2020; Kamarthi, Rodríguez, and Prakash 2021; Koemen et al. 2025; Wilsens, Antonio, and Claeskens 2025).
  • A possible emulator.
    • A model fine-tuned on epinowcast simulations may emulate epinowcast in a fraction of its run time.
    • We did not compare with epinowcast, so we cannot say how close it is.
    • The same approach could emulate other slow packages.
  • Further work.
    • Other foundation models.
    • More case studies.
    • A comparison with custom neural networks.
    • Testing the fine-tuned model as an emulator of epinowcast.
    • Fine-tuning with other epidemiological features as covariates, such as distances between locations or mobility.
    • Key references: (Das, Kong, et al. 2024; Woo et al. 2024; Rasul et al. 2023; Kamarthi, Rodríguez, and Prakash 2021; Koemen et al. 2025; KIT Metrics Lab, Bracher, and Schienle 2025).
  • Conclusions.
    • Generic forecasting models can be reused, with domain knowledge added through simulated data.
    • More case studies are needed before routine use.
